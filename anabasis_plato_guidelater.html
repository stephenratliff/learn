I

Introduction

Two texts, one question

In 399 BCE, Athens executed Socrates. Two of his devoted followers — Plato and Xenophon — responded to that catastrophe with opposite projects, yet both circle the same question the execution posed: what should a human being formed by philosophy do in a world that kills philosophers?

Plato withdrew. He founded the Academy and tried to transform the world by educating minds toward the eternal Good. Xenophon went to war. Two years after Socrates died, he joined a mercenary expedition into the Persian Empire — ten thousand Greeks hired by Cyrus the Younger to seize the throne from his brother Artaxerxes II. When Cyrus was killed at the battle of Cunaxa and the Greek generals were treacherously murdered, Xenophon found himself leading the army on a twelve-hundred-mile march home through hostile territory. The Anabasis — literally ἀνάβασις, "the march up" — is his account of that journey.

Read in isolation, the Anabasis is military history. Read against the Republic and the Timaeus, it becomes something else entirely: a philosophical testing-ground where Plato's most daring theories encounter the maximum possible friction from reality. The tripartite soul marches. The Demiurge gives speeches in front of exhausted soldiers. The allegory of the cave is traversed in boots, not in mind. And the errant cause of the Timaeus — the irreducible resistance of matter to reason — manifests as an Armenian blizzard that kills sentries where they stand.

II

The March

401–399 BCE — Click the map

In 401 BCE, Cyrus the Younger — Persian prince, satrap of Lydia, and rival to his brother Artaxerxes II — secretly hired roughly ten thousand Greek mercenaries to help him seize the Persian throne. Xenophon joined as a private person on the advice of a friend, with the distant blessing of Socrates. The true objective was concealed from the Greeks until they were deep into Asia Minor. At Cunaxa, near Babylon, the Greek right wing routed everything before them — but Cyrus, spotting his brother across the field, charged impulsively, was wounded, and was killed. The battle was won on the right; the war was lost in the center.

Then Tissaphernes, the Persian satrap, invited the Greek generals to a peace conference under sacred oaths and murdered them all. Ten thousand armed men without officers, surrounded by enemies, in the middle of a continent. The march north through the mountains of Kurdistan, across the Armenian plateau through deep winter, down to the Black Sea at Trapezus — is one of antiquity's most extraordinary stories. Xenophon, who began as a private citizen, became the march's philosophical and practical leader.

III

The Tripartite Soul & the Army

Republic IV & IX — Select an incident below

The Republic's most consequential psychological claim, developed in Books IV and IX, is that the soul has three distinct functional parts: the λογιστικόν (the reasoning, calculating faculty), the θυμοειδές (the spirited, honor-loving, fighting faculty), and the ἐπιθυμητικόν (the appetite-driven, pleasure-seeking faculty). A just soul, like a just city, is one where reason governs, spirit enforces reason's decrees, and appetite is given its proper portion and no more. Every form of injustice — and every form of political deterioration — corresponds to a different mismatch between these parts.

The army of the Anabasis is the tripartite soul made literal and set in motion under the worst possible conditions. The λογιστικόν is Xenophon (and the surviving officers) doing the work of deliberation, strategy, and speech-giving. The thumoeidetic mass is the ten thousand soldiers — fighters by trade, driven by honor, courage, and competitive Greek identity. The ἐπιθυμητικόν is the body of the army with its insistent biological needs: food, warmth, rest, plunder, women. These three elements are in constant negotiation, and the text's drama is largely the drama of their shifting proportions.

IV

Xenophon as Demiurge

Timaeus 29e–48e — Logos persuades necessity

The Timaeus introduces the Demiurge — the divine craftsman who brings the cosmos into being — through a formula that is also the key to Xenophon's leadership: the Demiurge does not compel the chaotic pre-cosmic material into order. He persuades necessity (ἀνάγκην … πείθουσα). The verb is crucial. The Demiurge works through logos, through rational suasion, coaxing the resistant material world toward the Good as far as its nature allows. This is not a god commanding obedience; it is a craftsman reasoning with his materials.

When Tissaphernes murders the generals, Xenophon's situation is the Demiurge's situation: a chaos-state — ten thousand armed men without leadership, surrounded by enemies, on alien ground — and the question of whether rational form can be imposed on it. Xenophon's response is not to seize authority by force but to speak. He convenes the surviving captains at night, deliberates, then addresses the wider army. The Anabasis is dense with these speeches, and they follow a recognizable demiurgic pattern: acknowledge the crisis; reframe it rationally; invoke divine sanction; project a logical path forward; appeal to the army's honor. The speech does not compel — the assembly can reject it, and sometimes does. It persuades.

Like the Timaeus's Demiurge, Xenophon cannot fully overcome the resistance of his material. The Timaeus is clear that the cosmos falls short of the eternal model because necessity is never fully mastered — the errant cause always retains some autonomy. The march similarly falls short: soldiers plunder when they should conserve, quarrel when they should coordinate, mutiny when they need discipline. Xenophon's logos wins more battles than it loses, but the army is what you get when a near-philosopher attempts Demiurgic reason on deeply imperfect material under maximum external pressure.

V

The Cave Inverted

Republic VII, 514a–521b — Two ascents

Plato's allegory of the cave in Republic Book VII is built around a word: the freed prisoner's journey from shadow to sunlight, from image to reality, is called an ἀνάβασις — an ascent. This is the same word Xenophon chose as his title. The overlap is too exact to be coincidence. Both texts are about a journey upward toward light, through suffering, by people who would rather stay where they are. The structural parallel is the organizing irony of everything that follows.

In the Republic, the philosopher who has seen the sun — who has beheld the Form of the Good — is reluctant to descend back into the cave. He must be compelled to govern. Xenophon presents himself in exactly this posture: when the army urges him to take command after the generals are killed, he insists he is only a private person, that others should lead, that he has no desire for authority. He must be persuaded to act. This reluctance is precisely the marker Plato gives the philosophical soul — the one who does not want power is the one who should have it.

The crucial difference is in what counts as light at the end of the tunnel. Plato's philosopher, ascending from the cave, beholds the Form of the Good — eternal, unchanging, beyond all material particulars. The army of the Anabasis ascends through mountains to the ridge of Mount Theches and sees — the Black Sea. A geographical feature. Salt water. The cry "Thalatta! Thalatta!" is the Anabasis's version of the moment the philosopher sees the sun, and the difference tells you everything about what separates Plato from Xenophon: one thinks philosophy transcends the human; the other thinks it proves itself within the human, among exhausted men on a mountain, looking at the sea.

VI

Necessity & the Errant Cause

Timaeus 47e–68d — Click each obstacle

The Timaeus constructs a dual causality for the cosmos. Everything real is the product of two cooperating (and sometimes conflicting) principles: νοῦς (mind, rational purposiveness) and ἀνάγκη (necessity, the errant cause — the irregular, wandering behavior of matter left to itself). The Demiurge produces order by persuading necessity to cooperate with reason as far as possible. But necessity always retains a residue of irreducible autonomy: matter does not simply obey mind. Fire burns without regard for human welfare. Disease strikes the virtuous. Earthquakes have no moral sensitivity. This is the planōmenē aitia — the "errant cause" — necessity operating outside rational governance.

The Anabasis is largely a sustained encounter with the errant cause made concrete. Mountains, rivers, blizzards, hostile tribes, treacherous diplomats — these are all necessity in its various forms, indifferent to Greek plans and moral claims. What makes Xenophon's response philosophically interesting is that he never treats this as cause for despair. He reads necessity as the frame within which logos must operate, never as logos's defeat. This is precisely the Timaean attitude: you do not rage at the errant cause; you persuade it where you can, and work around it where you cannot.

Timaeus Connection — 46c–e Timaeus distinguishes between causes that are "intelligent and the co-workers of the best" and those that "produce their scattered effects at random and without order." The two types cooperate in everything real. Xenophon's march enacts this distinction constantly: the intelligent causes are his speeches and plans; the co-causes operating randomly are terrain, weather, and the unpredictable actions of hostile peoples. Neither type alone produces the result — both are required, and their proper relationship requires the Demiurge to manage them.
VII

Justice in Extremis

Republic I–IV — Five dilemmas from the march

The Republic's central question — τί ἐστιν δικαιοσύνη (what is justice?) — is posed in the calm of a comfortable house in the Piraeus, with philosophical leisure and Socrates in no immediate danger of dying from cold or starvation. In the Anabasis, the same question is posed at knifepoint, in snowstorms, with hungry men and no food supply. The two texts produce between them the full philosophical range of justice: its abstract definition in the Republic, its practical exigencies in the Anabasis.

Plato's answer — that justice is each part of the soul and city performing its proper function without usurping another's role — translates into practice in the march as: reason should deliberate, soldiers should fight, appetites should be rationed. When these functions are disordered — when the assembly votes on military strategy (appetite/democracy), when a strong man forces a decision (tyranny of thumos), or when Xenophon himself overreaches — the march goes badly. The moments of genuine collective success correspond, almost without exception, to Xenophon's reason governing, the soldiers' spirit executing, and everyone's appetites being managed but not ignored.

Xenophon is put on trial twice by the army, accused of striking soldiers unjustly. His defense speeches in Book V are among the most vivid passages in the whole narrative: a commander justifying his exercise of authority to the very men he commands, submitting to their collective judgment. The outcome — the soldiers largely acquit him, some weep — suggests that justice in practice is a matter of lived relationship and recognized character, not merely formal rule-following. This is not a criticism of the Republic's theory; it is its vindication under the hardest possible conditions.

VIII

Cyrus & the Timocratic Soul

Republic VIII, 545c–550c

Book VIII of the Republic traces the downward sequence of souls and regimes from the best to the worst. Just below the ideal philosopher-king comes the timocratic man — the honor-loving, courageous, spirited type, governed by θυμός rather than λόγος. This type loves military victory, prizes reputation above all, is generous to friends but harsh to enemies, and is constitutionally incapable of subordinating his honor to rational policy. Plato's model for the timocratic city is Sparta. His model for the timocratic soul could have been Cyrus the Younger.

Xenophon's portrait of Cyrus is idealized: he is beautiful, brave, magnanimous, naturally commanding. His soldiers adore him. He pays well, keeps his word, shares hardship, never takes more than his due. Every quality Plato assigns to the spirited part of the soul at its best — courage, loyalty, competitive pride, love of honor — Cyrus has in abundance. He is everything the timocratic man should be, which is also Plato's point: why the timocratic man ultimately fails.

At Cunaxa, Cyrus has the battle won on the right. He needs only to wait. But he spots Artaxerxes across the field — his brother, his rival — and the sight triggers something beyond rational calculation. He charges directly at the king, kills one man who blocks him, wounds Artaxerxes himself, and is then cut down by a spear to the eye. The battle is won; the war is lost; ten thousand men are stranded. This is Plato's timocratic man meeting his constitutive fate: the thumoeidetic soul contains within its greatest virtue — the refusal to yield, the instinct to press advantage, the personal affront of his brother's presence — the seed of its own destruction. Reason would have said: wait. Thumos said: charge.

Republic Connection — Book VIII, 548e–549a Plato describes the timocratic man as one who "honors rulers and is ambitious, claiming that one ought to rule not on the basis of speaking or of some such thing, but on the basis of deeds of war." Cyrus is exactly this: he inspires loyalty through presence and action, not through reasoned persuasion. Contrast Xenophon, who leads almost entirely through speech. The march exists because a logos-leader had to replace a thumos-leader who destroyed himself at the moment of apparent victory.
IX

The Five Regimes

Republic VIII–IX — Click a character to place them

The Republic's sequence of declining regimes — from the ideal city (aristocracy of wisdom) through timocracy, oligarchy, democracy, to tyranny — provides a political taxonomy for the characters and situations of the Anabasis. The march is not a single regime; it is a moving encounter with all five simultaneously, in different people and different moments. The same army exhibits democratic deliberation in its assemblies, timocratic courage in battle, oligarchic resentment when rations are unequal, and something approaching tyranny when Xenophon must impose discipline by force.

X

Two Students of Socrates

The productive tension at the heart of ancient philosophy

Reading the Anabasis through the Republic and Timaeus finally forces the most important question: what did Socrates teach, and who inherited it rightly? Plato and Xenophon were both devoted to him. Both wrote defenses of him against Athenian charges. Both gave him the same fundamental intellectual character: the person who knows he does not know, who interrogates everything, who pursues the good relentlessly without claiming to have arrived. Yet their responses to his death produced radically different accounts of what philosophy is for.

Plato — ἡ Ἀκαδημία

Plato's answer: withdraw, teach, build an institution. The Academy is a protected space where minds can ascend from the cave without the constant distraction of bodily necessity. The philosopher must first see the Form of the Good — must complete the intellectual ἀνάβασις — before returning to govern.

The Timaeus's Demiurge is Plato's ideal: a being of pure reason that imposes form on chaos from the outside, guided by the eternal Forms as its model. The political world is the cave — real, dangerous, and philosophically inferior to what reason alone can grasp.

Xenophon — ἡ Στρατεία

Xenophon's answer: stay, act, endure. The march is a protected space for nothing — it is the most exposed possible test of whether philosophical education produces anything practically valuable. The philosopher proves himself not in contemplation but in command.

His Demiurge works from within the chaos, not above it. He does not see the Form of the Good before persuading the soldiers; he deliberates under fire with imperfect information. This is what philosophy looks like when it cannot wait for perfect conditions.

Neither position is simply superior. Plato is right that logos without philosophical formation is just rhetoric — and the army's worst moments confirm exactly what happens when thumos and epithumia go unsupervised by anything like philosophical education. Xenophon is right that philosophical formation without action is self-indulgent — and his march confirms that genuine practical wisdom, under sufficient pressure, produces something real: ten thousand men alive and home.

The most precise way to state the tension: Plato's ἀνάβασις is the mind's journey from appearance to reality, from the cave's shadows to the sunlight of the Forms. Xenophon's ἀνάβασις is the body's journey — through foreign darkness, over mountain passes, through blizzards and ambushes — toward salt water. Both achieve a kind of light. One discovers that the Good is eternal and cannot be lost. The other discovers that Greek soldiers are weeping at the sight of the Black Sea, and that this matters just as much.

What the Republic idealizes, the Anabasis pragmatizes. What the Timaeus theorizes about the soul's struggle against necessity, the Anabasis enacts in blood and snow. Together they mark out the full range of what philosophy, in the ancient Greek sense, thought it could and should do in the world — and the vast, productive tension between those ambitions.
