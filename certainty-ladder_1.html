02

Plato — the exception the doubt can't reach

Before Descartes ever doubted anything, Plato had already located truth somewhere the senses can't touch.

For Plato, what you see, hear, and touch is a flickering copy of something more real. A drawn triangle is never perfectly a triangle — its lines have width, its angles are slightly off — yet you can reason about a perfect triangle with total precision. That perfect triangle isn't in the world of chalk and paper; it's a Form, existing independently of any physical instance, grasped by reason rather than perception.

This is why Plato's Forms sit outside almost every later skeptical attack. Descartes can doubt a dream, an illusion, a deceiving demon — all of that doubt targets the senses. But a geometric truth doesn't depend on whether the physical world exists at all. It would hold in any possible world. That's not a coincidence; it's the entire point of the theory.

The senses show you shadows on a cave wall. Reason is the only thing that can turn you around to face the fire. — paraphrasing the allegory of the cave, Republic Book VII

Notice the shape of the move, because it recurs in every section below: whenever a philosopher wants to secure a truth against doubt, they reach for something senses-independent. Plato just made that move first, and most explicitly.

Descartes — the doubt cascade

Descartes doesn't doubt things one at a time out of curiosity. He's running a demolition, looking for the one belief that survives being demolished. Try it yourself: doubt each certainty below, in order.

The senses — I see a fire in front of me click to doubt
Dreams feel exactly this vivid while they're happening. If a dream can fool you completely, so can waking perception.
Mathematics — 2 + 3 = 5 click to doubt
Suppose a powerful deceiver has built your mind to be wrong even about simple arithmetic, every single time you check it. You'd have no way to notice.
The external world — that anything exists outside my mind click to doubt
Everything you've called "external" arrived through the senses you've already discredited. There's no independent route left to check it.
My own body — that I have hands, a head, a body at all click to doubt
A body is just another object in the external world you've already put in doubt. It falls with everything else.
That I am doubting, right now click to test
cogito, ergo sum

The trick isn't that Descartes finds something true. It's that he finds something self-defeating to deny. To doubt that you're doubting, you have to be doubting. The demon can fake a fire, fake arithmetic, fake a body — but it cannot fake the fact that an act of doubting is currently happening, because that act is the very thing producing the doubt. That's the single rung of the ladder no one below can knock out.

05

Chomsky — the ladder's undefined foundation

Chomsky studies language as a mental organ: a fixed, innate structure (Universal Grammar) that generates unbounded functions (actual sentences) once triggered by a child's environment. Structure precedes and produces function. On its face, that reads as biology, which reads as monism.

But Chomsky refuses to cash that reading in. His argument is historical: Descartes' dualism only made sense because Descartes had a sharp, working definition of "the physical" — matter as extended stuff that moves only by direct contact, push against push. Given that narrow picture of body, Descartes needed a second substance, res cogitans, to explain what mechanical contact plainly couldn't: open-ended, creative language use. Toggle the two views below.

In Descartes' physics, one body moves another only by touching it. That's the entire mechanism available to "the physical." Language — grammatically infinite, appropriate to context, un-triggered by any stimulus — looked like nothing contact-mechanics could produce. Hence: a second substance was required to house it.

Then Newton showed gravity acting across empty space, with no contact mechanism at all — a result Newton himself distrusted. Chomsky's point: "physical" was never redefined cleanly after that. It just came to mean "whatever successful physics currently explains," a category that keeps expanding. So asking "is thought physical" has no fixed target for "physical" to be measured against — not because the mind is mysterious, but because the word doing the measuring is undefined.

So Chomsky lands closer to mysterianism than to either classical label: not a second substance, but also not a confident claim that mind reduces to a "physical" we've never actually pinned down. Some problems, he suggests, may sit outside the reach of the particular cognitive organ doing the asking — the way a rat's mind is simply not the right shape for calculus, independent of whether calculus is real. On the ladder above, that's why his rung has no floor: he's not disputing where to stand on the ladder. He's disputing that the ladder's foundation was ever built.

06

Synthesis

Four different answers to where certainty comes from — and one refusal to accept that the question is well-formed.

ThinkerWhat grounds knowledgeRole of the sensesWhere they land
Plato Reason alone, grasping Forms that exist independent of any physical instance Actively misleading — sense objects are unstable copies of the real thing Pure reason, no exceptions
Descartes Doubt everything the senses report until reaching one self-certifying act of thought Distrusted first, rebuilt later once reason has secured a foundation Reason-first, empiricism rebuilt on top
Popper Bold conjectures (rational in origin) that only survive by surviving observation The only court that can kill a theory — but not the source of the theory itself Rationalist proposal, empiricist test
Chomsky Innate structure (a mental organ) that generates function once triggered by experience Triggers and shapes the organ, but doesn't build its structure from scratch Declines to answer — "physical" is undefined

Read top to bottom, the table looks like a march toward empiricism — reason gradually ceding ground to the senses. But Chomsky's row breaks the pattern rather than extending it. He isn't further along the reason-to-senses spectrum than Popper. He's questioning whether the spectrum has a well-defined endpoint on either side. Every earlier thinker on this ladder agreed, at least, on what "physical" or "sense-given" meant, even while disagreeing about how much to trust it. Chomsky's contribution is to point out that Newton already took that shared assumption away from everyone after Descartes — we just kept doing epistemology as though he hadn't.
